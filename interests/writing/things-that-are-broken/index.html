    Things that are broken

    The unholy odor of freshly wetted garbage wafted down the alley, slightly annoying the man. It was unpleasant. But at least Kolkata hasn’t turned into the goddamned Kalahari yet, he thought.

    He walked with a spring in his step, this otherwise disgruntled-looking man. There was a stubble on his face, and an altogether respectful amount of beer in his belly. Just enough to give him a spring in his walk, but not enough to make him stumble. Barely anything could make him stumble, you see. This here was a hard man, a man who fancied that he had seen it all - and liked none of it. Give him lemons, and he would save them for his weekly tequila. He had on one of those waxy-looking jeans one could find for 200 or so at New Market near the end of the day. Above that, a t-shirt that was perhaps brown in color. It might have said something along the lines of “I love NYC” at some point on it, but it had been eroded into only two disjoint splotches of white. Nothing to say, after all. He had never been to New York anyway.

    The wet garbage, as you may remember, was wetted by the first rain shower of the year. Cursing the Alipur Weather Department, like the millions of opinionated (and drunk) Bengali men making their way to a roll joint who came before him, the man ducked into a nondescript shop near the middle of the alley.

    He had no intention of buying anything; he only wanted to avoid the rain. His cynical self couldn’t help but wonder what inspires someone to set up shop in a place like this. They definitely won’t be getting a lot of traffic. The rest of the alley was shuttered, and a dog seemed to really be enjoying the day.

    The front of the shop gave the man pause. A short door, cracked in places due to age, separated the alley from the inside. The cracks were deep, and he spotted a lot of dirt in them. I wonder how long that dirt has been there. There was a small plant, barely clinging onto life with two tiny, minuscule leaves, that had taken root in one of those cracks. Maybe today was its lucky break.

    With no windows to peer inside, the man pushed the door open right away. He never had a sense of ceremony.

    Inside, it was quite dark. It was old, both in how it looked and how it smelled. A small room, perhaps about the size of the carrom room in his local sporting club. The walls were not grimy, but old cobwebs crusted in dust lined the top corners.

    He derived great pleasure from the idea that whoever maintained this shop must be mighty short and must have never heard of a dusting stick. The last comment was particularly enjoyable to him.

    The floor was speckled marble tiles, a design truly only appealing to the mid-twentieth-century Bengali. All around him, lit by two or maybe three terrifyingly yellow tungsten bulbs, were tables.

    Many sorts of tables - old mahogany tables, cracked linoleum tables, and the disgusting red plastic ones with a hole in the middle. There were many of them, so many that there was barely any way forward!

    On these tables, placed with evident care, were myriad things. Really, an absolute myriad. The man, now confused, thought he saw a Rubik’s cube, an old crusty-looking watch, a children’s slate, a bunch of pens of several different types, a dried-up succulent, and wait… is that an empty bottle of Absolut vodka? Intrigued and perhaps a bit scared, though he would never admit it, the man walked towards the table in front of him.

    He picked up the dusty Rubik’s cube on it and tried to recall all of those algorithms he learnt in the cubing club at school. Turning it around, he noticed that the center blue tile on the cube was gone - a broken cube is no good.

    A thin cough filled the room, and the man dropped the cube right onto the marble floor in shock.

    “Ki chai apnar? (What do you want?)” said the severely old man standing in the corner of the room. The man realized that he must have been there the entire time - he must be the owner.

    The man hurriedly explained the rain (the old man must be hard of hearing, he thought) and that he had just ducked into the store to spare himself a good drenching.

    The old man’s face betrayed no particular emotion. The man found comfort in this - he had always believed that a sane person is almost always a dispassionate one.

    The old man was clean-shaven, and wrinkly all over. His hair held the white fluffy nature of a roadkill pigeon.

    Greyish pyjamas and a wonderfully colorful shirt were on this person. The kind hippies wore to an orgy, you know.

    Faced with the dissonant, incoherent image of the old man before him, the man looked down at his feet and realized that the cube had promptly shattered—another tile missing. He hurriedly picked it up, and as he was about to ask for its price out of shame, the old man spoke in his surprisingly meek voice: “Don’t worry about it. A broken thing only gains in character once it is broken again.”

    What a curious thing to say, thought the man. Maybe this guy was an honest-to-god hippie from ye olden days. He had never met one, but always wondered why they were so happy.

    “What kind of shop is this - antiques?” asked the man.

    “I sell broken things,” replied the old man.

    A thousand sarcastic comments flooded our protagonist’s mind as he grappled to rein them in, for the old man may yet let him wait out the rain.

    “Fuck Alipur,” he mumbled as he blurted out the obvious next thing to ask. “Why?”

    “Because all broken things must have secrets in them,” answered the hippie.

    He was getting a bit annoyed, the man, that is. While a curious little shack in the old city with a strange old character in it was an appealing plot device, the dissonance that this drunk and happy man felt emanating from the oldie was a visceral sort of feeling. He swallowed it.

    Saying nothing in reply, for fear of engaging the strange man, he picked up the slate. It fell apart into two—the thing wasn’t even glued together. On the back, a small scribble declared a possible past owner, a child. ‘Ratal,’ it said, ‘Section A.’

    “Once there was a child, around 5 or 6, in a village in Hooghly. Bright kid, really liked the rumble of chalk on a slate. His brother gave him this slate as a present when he started school. I saw the child break it into two and chuck it into the pond. I think the school closed - some budget act?” the old man recited to the man. The buzzing of alcohol calming down, the man might have just ignored him. But he did see the remnants of a ‘ক’ character on the slate, clearly written down over and over again on the same spot with great care. Perhaps it will never get erased entirely.

    Surprising himself, the man reached for the cube again.

    “What about this thing?” he asked no one in particular.

    “Mukesh, I knew in school. Never interested in anything, the pessimist type. A teacher gave him a cube, that very one, when he scored well on a math test in 5th grade. He had cheated, of course, but he had the cube now nonetheless. Day and night he solved the cube - over and over. Gave him some sort of purpose, I wager. He ended up doing a PhD in something terribly complicated, last I heard. He left it behind in the school desk drawer when his family moved - I took it.”

    “People actually buy this shit,” the man could not hold it in anymore. The old man gently smiled at him.

    “Not as many as I would like, I am afraid,” he said.

    “Secrets are a precious commodity, but people rarely realize this. I would say nowadays, but they never really have,” he continued.

    Not understanding a word but feeling like he was in a headspace that had escaped him for a few decades, the man just asked:

    “What secret?”

    The old man walked towards him and held out his hand for the cube. The man handed it over, and the old man twisted it a few times into a more entropic mix.

    “See, it turns well even now. It was one of those magnetic ones, very pricey back then.”

    “You see how faded the colors on the tiles are? How scratched the corners are? That boy loved his cube,” he said.

    “But what secret! That is not a secret!” the man was taken a bit aback by how interested he felt in this.

    “Well, babu, it was a secret to you before. It is not anymore - this secret of Mukesh’s love for his cube. But it remains a secret to everyone else, you see?”

    The conviction with which this was said to him was a strange thing to experience. It was true, what the old man said. Perhaps a benign fact in a world full of them, but a fact nonetheless.

    He looked at the crusty watch with a dirt-filled embossing of ‘Love, Anjali’ around the dial. He did not ask a question.

    The rain had stopped, that much was clear to him. He looked at the old man, who was holding one of those mass-produced metal rickshaw showpieces they sell in Haridwar gift shops. It was missing a wheel. The old man looked content.

    “The rain has stopped. Ami elam, dada (I’ll take my leave),” said the man.

    As the door opened up and the light outside blinded him for a second, the man, for the first time in a few decades, thought of nothing but the smell of rain and wet garbage.

    He turned around, and his eyes descended to meet the poor two-leaf plant on the door. The splashes had taken one of the leaves off.

    He bent down and touched the cold, wet, sole surviving leaf with a tenderness that his friends did not know him for.

    He smiled.

    He wondered why he ever thought that the world was a sphinx without a secret. He told himself that he would remember the leaf. Perhaps, the leaf would remember something of him.
